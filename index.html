How the calculator works

The calculator normalizes your input to a fully automatic timing (FAT), static-start baseline using small adjustments for timing method (about +0.24 seconds for track-style hand times) and start type. It then scales to other distances using reference proportions for a competitive male athlete.

Sprint performance is not linear. Short sprints depend more on acceleration; longer sprints depend more on max velocity and speed endurance. Estimates are rounded and presented with a confidence rating so you can see how reliable they are for your inputs.

Why sprint conversions are not exact

  • Sprinting is not linear — acceleration, top speed, and speed endurance each play different roles at different distances.
  • Hand-timed and phone-video runs typically read faster than FAT or laser systems.
  • Block, 3-point, and standing starts all begin from rest. Rolling and flying starts skip part or all of the acceleration phase.
  • Surface, footwear, wind, and weather change real-world results.
  • One time alone is a rough sample; splits and a measured top speed make estimates more reliable.

FAQ

How accurate is the sprint time calculator?

It returns estimates, not official marks. Sprint performance is not linear, and timing method, start type, surface, and conditions all change real results. The confidence rating tells you how strong the estimate is for your inputs.

What is the difference between hand time and FAT?

Hand-timed runs are typically 0.20–0.30 seconds faster than fully automatic timing because of human reaction delay starting the watch. The calculator can apply a +0.24-second adjustment to estimate a FAT equivalent for track-style hand times. For short combine-style tests, timing systems vary, so the adjustment is only an estimate.

Why does start type matter?

Block, 3-point, and standing starts all begin from rest. Rolling and flying starts skip the acceleration phase, so a flying 40 will look much faster than the same effort from a standing or 3-point start. Mixing start types reduces confidence in cross-distance comparisons.

Are the benchmarks an exact percentile?

No. Benchmarks are general performance ranges drawn from cited sources. They are useful as broad guidance but should not be treated as percentile rankings.

Will this help with recruiting?

This site does not predict, guarantee, or otherwise determine recruiting outcomes. It can give you a quick directional estimate to share with a coach or training partner. Talk to a qualified coach for individualized advice.
