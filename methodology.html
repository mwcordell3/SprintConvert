Methodology

How estimates are produced, what they assume, and where the limits are.

Sprinting is not linear

Sprint performance is the result of three overlapping qualities: acceleration, maximum velocity, and speed endurance. Short sprints (10 to 30 yards) are dominated by acceleration. Mid-range sprints (40 to 60 yards, 60 meters) include the early max-velocity phase. Longer sprints (100 to 200 meters) add a speed-endurance demand because athletes cannot maintain peak velocity all the way through.

This means a single time at one distance does not perfectly predict times at other distances. The calculator scales between distances using reference proportions for a competitive male athlete with fully automatic timing (FAT) and a static start. Results become less precise the further the conversion has to extrapolate.

Short sprints measure acceleration more heavily

A 10- or 20-yard time mostly reflects how quickly an athlete builds speed. Two athletes with the same 10-yard time can have very different 100-meter times because their max velocity and ability to hold it differ.

Longer sprints depend on max velocity and speed endurance

From 60 meters onward, the slowdown phase becomes important. Even elite sprinters lose a few percent of their peak velocity by the end of a 100-meter race. The calculator's reference proportions account for this, but a measured top-speed input or a flying-segment time produces a more reliable longer-distance estimate.

Timing method changes results

Hand stopwatches typically read 0.20 to 0.30 seconds faster than fully automatic timing. The calculator uses a +0.24-second adjustment for track-style hand times to estimate a FAT equivalent. For short combine-style tests the real difference can vary more, so the adjustment is presented as an estimate. Phone-video timing has frame-rate uncertainty and start-detection error and is treated as low confidence regardless of distance.

Start type changes results

Block, 3-point, and standing starts all begin from rest, with small differences in the first few yards. Rolling and flying starts skip the acceleration phase, so a flying 40-yard time will look much faster than a stand-alone 40 from the same effort. The calculator normalizes the input to a static-start baseline so cross-distance comparisons are honest.

Hand time is not the same as FAT

Even with the +0.24-second adjustment applied, a converted hand-time figure is an estimate, not an official mark. Treat it as directional information and record both numbers (the original hand time and the converted estimate) when sharing results.

Benchmarks are broad general ranges

The benchmark dataset is intentionally small. It only includes cited general ranges where we have a clear public source — football 40-yard dash, baseball 60-yard dash, and soccer 30-meter sprint. We do not yet publish a 100-meter general-population benchmark, female-specific tables, or fine-grained age-specific ranges because we do not have strong cited sources for them and we would rather show no benchmark than show a misleading one.

No benchmark is an exact percentile

A reported "Advanced" or "Elite" tier in this calculator is a broad guideline. It does not mean the athlete is in any specific percentile of any specific population, and it does not predict recruiting or roster outcomes.

Why we prioritize transparency over fake precision

Many sprint calculators show two-decimal estimates with no caveats. We deliberately round, attach a confidence rating to every result, and flag inputs that limit reliability (phone video, flying starts, single-time entries, weak source data). The aim is to be more useful than thin calculator pages by being more honest about what the numbers mean.

Inputs we deliberately don't take

We do not collect names, schools, exact birthdays, locations, or contact details. Athlete profile fields use broad ranges (age 13–14, 15–16, 17–18, college / adult; sex / category; sport; position) so the calculator stays useful without storing personal data. See the privacy page for more.
